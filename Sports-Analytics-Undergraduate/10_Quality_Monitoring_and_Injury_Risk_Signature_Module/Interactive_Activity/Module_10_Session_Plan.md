# Module 10 Session Plan: Quality Monitoring and Injury Risk

**Format:** one 3-hour class session, three segments, two 10-minute breaks.

## Segment 1 (50 minutes): What a control chart is, and where this module comes from

- I'll open by telling the class this module is different: it comes from my own published research, not the textbook, and walk through why I study this question (can an athlete's own data flag trouble before it becomes an injury).
- Short lecture: introduce the control chart idea in plain terms before any code, center line, control limits, special-cause signal.
- Video: the plain-language control chart explainer, before touching real data.
- Live coding cue: we'll open RStudio together and build Joel Embiid's real per-game shot attempt series from the real 2023-24 shot log.
- I'll walk through the real control limit formulas together with the class, computing his real center line, moving range, and control limits live.

**Break (10 minutes).**

## Segment 2 (50 minutes): Reading the real signal, and what it does and does not prove

- Live coding cue: we'll flag Embiid's real out-of-control game together and I'll show the real timeline next to his actual reported injury and return dates.
- Cold call: before I reveal the real date of his flagged game, I'll ask the class to guess how close, if at all, it falls to his actual missed-game stretch.
- Class discussion (talking points in speaker notes, not slides): what this one real case does prove, what it does not prove, and why a single flagged game is a prompt for a conversation, not a diagnosis.
- Pair discussion: each pair picks a different real current or recent player they know missed time to injury and discusses what workload signal might have been worth watching.

**Break (10 minutes).**

## Segment 3 (50 minutes): The ACWR, its real weaknesses, and closing out the course

- Short lecture: introduce the Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio and why it is the most common tool in this space.
- Live coding cue: none needed here, this segment works directly from the real Kanwal et al. (2025) review rather than new computation.
- I'll walk the class through each real documented weakness: mathematical coupling, arbitrary windows, weak early evidence, and the real null result from the youth football randomized controlled trial.
- Interactive activity: small groups each take one real ACWR weakness and prepare a thirty-second real explanation of it to the rest of the class, as if briefing a front office that is considering adopting it.
- Closing: I'll tie this module back to the whole course, ending on the habit of checking a framework's own math before trusting it, in workload monitoring or anywhere else in this field.

## Before next class

Lab 10, the Module 10 quiz, and the practice questions. This closes out our ten-module sequence.
