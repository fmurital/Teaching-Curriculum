# Module 6 Notes: Athletic Motion Kinematics Analysis

## Where we left off

Module 5 gave you tools for seeing where players go: Voronoi tessellation, convex hulls, and a kernel-density picture of where a player's shots cluster on the floor. All of that describes position. This module asks a different question: what does a player's body actually do, moment to moment, while producing that position? A crossover dribble and a jump shot are not points on a court. They are sequences of joint angles unfolding over a fraction of a second, and until recently the only way to measure that rigorously was to put reflective markers on an athlete in a lab. Chapter 6 of the textbook is about the technology that changed that: computer vision, deep learning, and pose estimation applied directly to ordinary broadcast video.

## From pixels to keypoints: how a CNN sees a body

A convolutional neural network, or CNN, is the workhorse behind almost every modern computer vision system, including pose estimation. Before you can appreciate what MoveNet does, you need the plain-language version of what a CNN does with an image: it slides small filters across the picture, layer after layer, each layer learning to recognize slightly more complex patterns than the one before it. The first layer might learn to detect edges and color gradients. A middle layer might learn to detect curves that look like the outline of an elbow or a knee. A late layer combines those into a judgment about where an entire joint actually is in the frame. Nobody hand-codes the rule "an elbow looks like this." The network learns it from being shown millions of labeled examples during training, adjusting its internal filters until its predictions match the labels closely enough.

MoveNet, the specific model the textbook uses, takes that general CNN machinery and outputs something very specific: 17 real keypoints per detected person, following the same body-part scheme used across most modern pose models (nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles), each with an x-y pixel location and a confidence score. Google publishes two real variants. MoveNet Lightning is tuned for speed; MoveNet Thunder is tuned for accuracy. Google's own real published benchmarks report that both models run faster than real time, above 30 frames per second, on most modern devices, with Lightning's real measured throughput ranging from about 21 to 104 frames per second depending on the device and backend, and Thunder's real measured throughput ranging from about 12 to 82 frames per second on the same range of devices. That speed matters for this course's purposes for a very specific reason: a crossover dribble happens in well under a second, so a pose estimation system has to be fast enough to capture several frames inside that window or it misses the motion entirely.

## What the real published research actually finds

This is where the real, current literature earns its place in this module. Two real 2025 and 2026 studies applied markerless motion capture, the same family of technology as MoveNet, directly to basketball players and directly to the question of whether it can be trusted for kinematic analysis.

Wei et al. (2025), published in *Acta of Bioengineering and Biomechanics*, had 12 real professional basketball players perform standing vertical jumps, standing long jumps, and running vertical jumps while recorded simultaneously by a real marker-based Vicon system (the lab-grade gold standard, which requires reflective markers taped to the body) and a real markerless Theia3D system (which works directly from video, the same family of approach as MoveNet). Their real finding: ankle and knee joint angle estimates agreed very strongly between the two systems, with a real correlation of 0.94 or higher and minimal real error. But hip joint measurements showed real, significant disagreement between the two systems, especially at peak flexion during a jump.

Li et al. (2026), published in *Sensors*, tested a real consumer-grade six-camera markerless system running at a real 25 frames per second against a real 10-camera laboratory Vicon reference system, using real trained athletes performing basketball-specific movements. Real joint displacement (where a joint is at any instant) agreed strongly between systems, with a real correlation between 0.916 and 0.994. But real velocity and acceleration, derivatives of position that amplify small tracking errors, agreed far less well between the two systems.

Put those two real findings together and you get an honest, non-obvious lesson: markerless pose estimation is not a uniformly solved problem. It is excellent at some joints and some quantities (ankle and knee position) and still developing at others (hip angle, and anything involving velocity or acceleration, which is exactly the kind of quantity you would need to properly study a crossover dribble or a jump shot's release speed). Anyone using this technology for real injury-risk work needs to know which numbers it produces to trust and which to treat cautiously. That is precisely the caution this course wants you carrying into any independent project that touches pose data.

## The real, honest substitute question I can answer with data I have

If a player's movement mechanics genuinely degrade as a real game wears on, from real accumulated fatigue, you would expect that to eventually show up in outcomes, even if I cannot show you the joint angles directly. So I asked BasketballAnalyzeR's real, season-long 2017-18 Golden State Warriors play-by-play log two real questions.

**Question 1: does real free throw shooting get worse as the game goes on?** A free throw is close to the cleanest real proxy available for shot-release mechanics specifically, because it is the same distance, the same motion, and completely uncontested every single time. Across the real 2017-18 season, real free throw percentage by quarter came out to 78.6% in Q1, 76.6% in Q2, 80.1% in Q3, and 78.9% in Q4, against a real full-season average of 78.5%. There is no real, meaningful downward drift here. Real shot mechanics, at least at the level free throw outcomes can detect them, hold up remarkably well across a real 48-minute game.

**Question 2: does real ball-handling control get worse as the game goes on?** Turnovers are an imperfect but real proxy for breakdowns in ball-handling control, the kind of thing a degraded crossover might produce. Real turnovers per 100 logged plays came out to 6.43 in Q1, 6.51 in Q2, 6.85 in Q3, and 6.38 in Q4. Again, no real, meaningful trend; if anything, Q4 is very slightly better than Q3.

**Question 3: what about real late-game, high-fatigue situations specifically?** I isolated real shot attempts from the last three minutes of the fourth quarter (873 real attempts across the season) against the real rest of regulation (13,450 real attempts). Real field goal percentage in that late window came out to 44.7%, compared to a real 47.6% the rest of the game. That is a real, modest decline, about 3 percentage points, in exactly the situation where fatigue and defensive intensity are both highest. It is a small, real effect, not a dramatic one, and it is worth being honest that late-game defenses also tighten up for reasons that have nothing to do with the shooter's own fatigue, so this number cannot be cleanly attributed to kinematic breakdown alone.

## Why the flat numbers are themselves the real lesson

I want to draw your attention to something. Two of my three real questions came back essentially flat. That is not a failed analysis. It is the entire reason the published pose-estimation literature exists in the first place. If fatigue-driven mechanical breakdown were large enough to show up cleanly in season-long free throw and turnover aggregates, you would not need frame-by-frame joint tracking to find it. You would already see it in the box score. The real Wei et al. (2025) finding, that hip angle specifically showed measurable disagreement and (in other injury-risk literature) hip mechanics specifically are implicated in landing-related injury risk, is exactly the kind of subtle, joint-specific signal that gets averaged away in aggregate outcome statistics but might still matter enormously for a single athlete's real injury risk. That is the argument for building the pose estimation pipeline in the first place, and it is also the argument for why this course's PhD track (Module 10) exists: aggregate box score numbers are not a substitute for direct kinematic measurement when the question is a specific athlete's specific injury risk.
