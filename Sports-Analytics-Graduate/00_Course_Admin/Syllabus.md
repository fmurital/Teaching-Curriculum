## Course Description

This course develops advanced, applied basketball analytics using real NBA data and the R programming language, extending descriptive box-score analysis into lineup-level splits, situational and clutch performance, spatial and player-tracking data, computer-vision methods for motion and object detection, and machine-learning models for scoring probability. Students build toward an independent final project applying these methods to a team, player, or dataset of their choosing. The course closes with a signature module connecting the semester's statistical-process-control and modeling tools to athlete workload and injury-risk detection, the instructor's own research area, where students pursuing a larger independent or PhD-level project go furthest.

## Course Learning Outcomes

- Build and interpret lineup-level splits, isolating any five-player combination's statistics from full-season play-by-play data.
- Compute and interpret clutch and situational performance splits and season-segment trends.
- Produce spatial visualizations of on-court data: animated plots, Voronoi diagrams, convex hulls, and gravity/distraction metrics.
- Apply computer-vision and deep-learning methods, including pose estimation and object detection, to athletic motion and ball-tracking data.
- Build performance maps through court segmentation and interpret classification trees (CART) fit to spatial data.
- Fit and evaluate machine-learning models, including random forests and their variants, for scoring-probability estimation.
- Apply statistical process control and workload-monitoring methods to athlete injury-risk data, and connect a real risk indicator to a specific, cited model.
- Read, evaluate, and incorporate current peer-reviewed sports-analytics research alongside the course textbook.
- Communicate an independent data analysis clearly in written and presented form, at a standard suitable for graduate-level work.

## Policy on the Usage of Artificial Intelligence

**AI use is allowed, but not required.** You are welcome to use AI tools in this course, including for writing R code, for any purpose. You should know that AI tools can produce incorrect facts, fabricated citations, and code that runs but produces the wrong answer, especially on the kind of layered, lineup-level, and model-fitting tasks this course covers. You are responsible for any inaccurate, biased, or otherwise faulty content you submit, regardless of whether it came from you or from an AI tool. If you use an AI tool for any part of an assignment, project, or quiz, its contribution must be credited in your submission (what you used it for and what you changed or verified yourself). Submitting AI-assisted work without that acknowledgment is treated as academic dishonesty under the KSU Code of Academic Integrity, the same as any other uncredited source.
